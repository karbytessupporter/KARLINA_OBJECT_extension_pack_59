Inflation (in the usual economic sense) means that the purchasing power of a unit of money decreases over time, so prices for goods and services rise on average. Economists usually describe inflation as being driven by several broad mechanisms rather than a single cause.

Below are the main causes.

1. Increase in the Money Supply

If the total amount of money in circulation grows faster than the supply of goods and services, each unit of currency represents a smaller share of real economic output.

Mechanism:

– Central banks create new money (for example through asset purchases or lending to banks).

– Governments run large deficits financed by money creation.

– More money chases the same amount of goods.

Result:

Prices rise because sellers know buyers have more money available.

A classic example is when a central bank like the Federal Reserve expands the money supply during economic stimulus programs.

2. Demand-Pull Inflation

This occurs when consumer demand grows faster than production capacity.

Examples:

– Economic boom

– Large government spending

– Increased consumer borrowing

Example scenario

– Many people suddenly want houses.

– Construction cannot keep up.

– House prices rise.

This is sometimes summarized as:

“Too much money chasing too few goods.”

3. Cost-Push Inflation

Inflation can also happen when the cost of producing goods rises, forcing businesses to raise prices.

Common drivers include:

– Higher wages

– Increased raw material costs

– Energy price spikes

– Supply chain disruptions

Example:

– Oil prices rise.

– Transportation becomes more expensive.

– Many products become more expensive.

4. Supply Shocks

When the supply of goods suddenly drops, prices rise even if demand stays the same.

Examples:

– Natural disasters

– Wars

– Pandemic disruptions

– Trade restrictions

A well-known example was the oil supply shocks of the 1970s.

5. Inflation Expectations

If businesses and consumers expect inflation, they behave in ways that actually cause it.

Examples:

– Workers demand higher wages anticipating higher prices.

– Businesses raise prices early to stay ahead of costs.

This creates a self-reinforcing cycle sometimes called a wage-price spiral.

6. Currency Devaluation

If a country’s currency loses value relative to others, imports become more expensive.

This raises prices for:

– fuel

– electronics

– raw materials

– food imports

7. Extreme Cases: Hyperinflation

In extreme situations, inflation can spiral out of control when governments print large amounts of money to finance spending.

Historical examples include:

– Weimar hyperinflation

– Zimbabwe hyperinflation

In such cases, prices can double within days or hours.

✅ In summary

Inflation usually results from a combination of:

– expanding money supply

– rising demand

– increasing production costs

– supply disruptions

– expectations of future inflation

Rather than one single cause.

M ≈ L ⋅ (NB + G)

That is the key result.

It means the money supply is not an ever-growing pile. It is more like a moving window of recently issued currency.

If you increase lifespan L, total active money rises.

If you increase daily issuance NB+G, total active money rises.

– real output falls
