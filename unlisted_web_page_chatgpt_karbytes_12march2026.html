Limited colors:

– Memes and reaction images

– Simple web animations

MP4 is a modern video container format designed for efficient digital video storage and streaming.

Millions of colors:

Audio support:

Smaller file sizes:

Typical uses:

– Movies and video clips

[plain-text]

Frame 1: full image

Frame 3: only pixels that changed
Frame 4: only pixels that changed

[plain-text]

[end plain-text]

Certain sounds—especially cymbal crashes, applause, and crowd noise—are difficult for MP3 compression because they contain very complex frequency structures that change extremely rapidly.

To understand why, we need to look at two ideas:

How MP3 identifies “important signal” vs “discardable noise.”

1. How MP3 Determines What Is “Significant”

MP3 compression relies on a psychoacoustic model—a mathematical model of how human hearing works.

The encoder analyzes each short slice of sound and asks:

“Which parts of this sound can a human actually hear?”

Anything predicted to be inaudible can be removed.

The encoder performs three main steps:

Step 1 — Convert waveform to frequency components
The audio frame is transformed into frequency bands.

Conceptually:

[plain-text]

frequency band      energy
100 Hz              strong
200 Hz              medium
300 Hz              weak
1000 Hz             strong

[end plain-text]

This gives the encoder a spectrum of the sound.

Step 2 — Estimate masking effects
Human hearing has a phenomenon called auditory masking.

A loud sound at one frequency can hide quieter nearby frequencies.

Example:

[plain-text]

1000 Hz tone (loud)
1010 Hz tone (very quiet)

[end plain-text]

The second tone may be inaudible because the first one masks it.

The encoder calculates a masking threshold:

masking threshold curve
Any frequencies below that curve are considered inaudible.

Those components can be removed or heavily compressed.

Step 3 — Allocate bits to audible components
Remaining frequencies above the masking threshold are treated as significant signal.

The encoder distributes bits to represent them:

[plain-text]

important frequencies → more bits
less important frequencies → fewer bits
masked frequencies → removed

[end plain-text]

Why Ray Tracing Works Even Though Light Travels the Opposite Direction

In the real world, light travels from sources (like the Sun or lamps) to objects and then into your eyes. But ray tracing algorithms send rays from the camera into the scene. Surprisingly, this produces the same result. The reason is a physical principle known as reciprocity in light transport.

1. Light Paths Work Both Directions

If a photon can travel along a path like this:

light source → wall → mirror → eye
Then the same path is physically valid in reverse:

eye → mirror → wall → light source
The laws of optics—reflection, refraction, and scattering—are time-reversible under normal conditions.

This idea is closely related to the principle of reciprocity in physics.

2. Why Reversing the Path Is Useful

In a scene with many light sources and surfaces, light may bounce millions of times. If we simulated light forward from the sources, most rays would never reach the camera.

Example:

[plain-text]

light emitted
↓
bounces randomly
↓
misses the camera entirely

[end plain-text]

That means enormous computational waste.

Instead, ray tracing asks:

“Which light paths actually contribute to what the viewer sees?”

So it starts at the camera.

3. Only Rays That Matter Are Traced

When a ray is sent from a camera pixel:

[plain-text]

camera pixel
↓
trace ray
↓
find object intersection

[end plain-text]

the algorithm is automatically exploring only the paths that could influence that pixel.

This dramatically reduces computation.

4. The Rendering Equation

The underlying mathematics describing light transport in computer graphics is the Rendering Equation.

In simplified form, it states that the light leaving a surface equals:

[plain-text]

emitted light
+ reflected incoming light from all directions

[end plain-text]

Ray tracing solves this equation by sampling paths through the scene.

[plain-text]

camera → surface → random bounce → wall → light
camera → surface → reflection → light

[end plain-text]

Over time, averaging many paths approximates the correct lighting.

– reflection angles

– refraction angles

– energy conservation

Your earlier idea about simulated time vs. physical time leads directly into how computers approximate physics. Real physics operates in continuous space and time, but computers must represent everything using discrete steps. Game engines and simulations therefore approximate the continuous universe using numerical methods.

1. Continuous Time in Physics

In classical physics, motion is described with continuous variables. For example, position changes continuously with time:

v = dx / dt

This equation means velocity is the rate of change of position with respect to time. The derivative

dx/dt

assumes time flows smoothly and infinitely divisible.

But computers cannot represent infinitely many time instants.

2. Discrete Time in Simulations

Instead of continuous time, simulations divide time into small steps.

A computer approximates the derivative with a finite difference:

v ≈ (x(t + Δt) − x(t)) / Δt
​
The simulation repeatedly advances the system:

state(t + Δt) = update(state(t))

Each update computes the next state of the world.

3. Numerical Integration

To simulate motion, engines use methods from numerical analysis. The simplest is Euler integration:

x_{t+\Delta t} = x_t + v_t\Delta t

This equation estimates the new position by assuming velocity stays constant during the small time interval.

More advanced engines use improved integrators (like Verlet or Runge–Kutta) to reduce error.

4. Why Small Time Steps Matter

Because the equations are approximations, simulations become more accurate when:

Δt becomes smaller

However smaller steps require more computation.

So game engines choose a compromise, often:

Δt ≈ 1/60 second

meaning the physics simulation updates about 60 times per second.

5. Discrete Space Representation

Space is also approximated in several ways.

Common methods include:

Polygon Meshes
Objects are represented by triangles forming surfaces.

Voxel Grids
Some simulations divide space into small cubes called voxels.

Particle Systems
Fluids or smoke may be represented as many moving particles.

These are all approximations of continuous geometry.

6. Why This Works Surprisingly Well

Even though reality is continuous, discrete approximations work because:

– Physical changes over very small time intervals are small.

– Errors accumulate slowly when time steps are small.

– Human perception tolerates small inaccuracies.

So the simulation can look convincing even though it is an approximation.

7. The Simulation Loop

Most games run a loop like this:

[plain-text]

while game_running:

    measure real elapsed time

    update physics using Δt

    update game logic

    render frame

[end plain-text]

This creates the illusion of a continuously evolving world.

8. A Conceptual Parallel to Physics

Interestingly, some theoretical physicists have speculated that space-time itself might be discrete at extremely small scales, possibly near the Planck length.

However, current physics still treats space-time as continuous.

Computer simulations simply approximate continuity using discrete steps.

✅ Key idea

Real physics:

continuous space + continuous time

Computer simulations:

discrete space + discrete time steps

Yet when the steps are small enough, the simulation behaves very similarly to a continuous system.

Great question. Monte Carlo path tracing is one of the most important techniques used to simulate realistic lighting in modern graphics. It combines ideas from probability, physics, and parallel computation on GPUs.

1. The Fundamental Problem: Light Comes From Everywhere

In reality, the light reaching a point on a surface depends on light arriving from every direction in the hemisphere above that surface.

Mathematically, the outgoing light intensity L_o from a surface depends on integrating incoming light over all directions:

L_o(x,\omega_o)=\int_{\Omega} f_r(x,\omega_i,\omega_o),L_i(x,\omega_i),(\omega_i\cdot n),d\omega_i

This equation is the light transport integral underlying the rendering equation.

Conceptually it says:

For each incoming direction ω_i

take the incoming light L_i

multiply by how the material reflects it f_r

sum over all directions.

The difficulty is that there are infinitely many directions.

Computing the integral exactly is impossible.

2. Monte Carlo Sampling

Instead of computing every direction, the renderer randomly samples a few directions.

This is called Monte Carlo integration.

The integral above is approximated by:

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{f_r(\omega_k)L_i(\omega_k)(\omega_k\cdot n)}{p(\omega_k)}

Here:

N = number of sampled directions

p(ω_k) = probability of choosing that direction

More samples → more accurate lighting.

3. What “Path Tracing” Means

A path is a sequence of light bounces.

Example path:

[plain-text]

camera → wall → floor → mirror → light

[end plain-text]

The algorithm randomly explores many such paths.

Each sampled path contributes some brightness to the final pixel.

4. Noise vs Accuracy

Because paths are random, the image initially looks noisy.

Example progression:

[plain-text]

10 samples per pixel  → very noisy
100 samples           → less noise
1000 samples          → nearly smooth

[end plain-text]

Noise decreases roughly with the square root of the sample count:

noise ∝ 1/sqrt(N)

This is why realistic rendering often requires many samples per pixel.

5. Why GPUs Are Perfect for This
GPUs contain thousands of small processing cores.

Each core can compute different rays independently.

Example parallel workload:

[plain-text]

GPU core 1 → pixel 1 path samples
GPU core 2 → pixel 2 path samples
GPU core 3 → pixel 3 path samples
...

[end plain-text]

Millions of rays can be simulated simultaneously.

This is called massively parallel computation.

6. Importance Sampling

Random sampling can be inefficient.

For example, rays aimed toward a light source matter more than rays aimed into darkness.

So renderers bias the sampling toward more important directions.

This technique is called importance sampling.

Example:

[plain-text]

more rays → toward bright lights
fewer rays → toward dark areas
This dramatically reduces noise.

[end plain-text]

7. Bounding Volume Acceleration
To make ray tracing faster, scenes are organized into spatial structures.

Common structure:

Bounding Volume Hierarchy

A BVH groups objects into hierarchical boxes.

Ray intersection checks proceed like:

    shoot primary ray

    while ray hits surface:

        sample new bounce direction
        accumulate light contribution
        probabilistically terminate path

[end plain-text]

The final pixel color is the average contribution of many paths.

What “Path Tracing” Means

Path tracing is a rendering algorithm used in computer graphics to simulate how light travels through a scene by randomly sampling many possible light paths.

A path is the route light might take from a light source to the camera after bouncing off surfaces.

Example path:

[plain-text]

light source → wall → floor → object → camera

[end plain-text]

A renderer generates many such paths and averages their contributions to determine the final pixel color.

Key characteristics of path tracing:

– rays originate from the camera

– each ray randomly bounces through the scene

– light contributions are accumulated

– averaging many paths approximates realistic lighting

Because it simulates multiple light bounces, path tracing naturally produces:

– soft shadows

– reflections

– refractions

– indirect lighting

– color bleeding between surfaces

This makes it one of the most physically accurate rendering techniques currently used in graphics.

Why It’s Called “Path” Tracing

Traditional ray tracing mainly follows direct rays:

[plain-text]

camera → object → light

[end plain-text]

Path tracing extends this by allowing many random bounces:

[plain-text]

camera → surface → random bounce → surface → random bounce → light

[end plain-text]

Each bounce represents a possible path a photon might take in reality.

The renderer explores thousands or millions of such paths.

The Deep Physics Connection: “Sum Over All Paths”

Your earlier curiosity about waves and signals connects to an important concept in physics known as the path integral formulation of quantum mechanics, developed by Richard Feynman.

In this formulation, a particle does not follow just one trajectory between two points. Instead, the probability of its motion depends on all possible paths connecting those points.

Mathematically, the probability amplitude is written as:

\langle x_f,t_f | x_i,t_i \rangle = \int \mathcal{D}[x(t)], e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}S[x(t)]}

This equation represents a sum over every possible path x(t) a particle could take.

Each path contributes with a phase determined by the action S[x(t)].

Why Only Certain Paths Matter

Although infinitely many paths exist, most cancel each other out due to wave interference.

Paths near the classical trajectory reinforce each other.

This leads to the classical principle of least action:

\delta S = 0

which determines the path that classical physics predicts.

Surprising Similarity to Path Tracing

There is a conceptual analogy between the two ideas:

Quantum physics Computer graphics

[plain-text]

particle explores all possible paths ----------------| renderer samples many light paths
contributions interfere	contributions ---------------| are averaged
dominant paths produce observable behavior ----------| dominant paths produce visible lighting

[end plain-text]

Both approaches deal with enormous numbers of possible paths through space and approximate the result by sampling.

Why Path-Traced Images Need Billions of Rays

At first it might seem surprising: the human retina contains roughly 6–7 million cone photoreceptors, yet realistic path-traced images often require billions of simulated rays. The reason is that a renderer must estimate extremely complex lighting interactions that the eye itself does not compute directly.

Let’s break down why so many rays are needed.

1. Pixels vs Eye Receptors

A modern display might contain millions of pixels.

Example:

[plain-text]

Resolution -------| Pixels
1080p ------------| ~2 million
4K ---------------| ~8 million
8K ---------------| ~33 million

[end plain-text]

Each pixel requires a lighting estimate.

But one ray per pixel is not enough, because the lighting equation requires sampling many directions.

2. Every Surface Receives Light From Many Directions
The brightness of a point on a surface depends on incoming light from all directions in the hemisphere above it.

L_o(x,\omega_o)=\int_{\Omega} f_r(x,\omega_i,\omega_o)L_i(x,\omega_i)(\omega_i\cdot n)d\omega_i

That integral represents infinitely many incoming directions.

A renderer approximates this using random sampling.

3. Many Samples Per Pixel

Instead of computing the full integral, the renderer evaluates it using random samples:

[plain-text]

Samples per pixel -------------| Image quality
1 -----------------------------| extremely noisy
10 ----------------------------| noisy
100 ---------------------------| decent
1000+ -------------------------| high quality

[end plain-text]

For a 4K image:

8 million pixels × 1000 samples
≈ 8 billion rays

And that is only the first bounce.

4. Rays Spawn More Rays
Each time a ray hits a surface, new rays may be created:

[plain-text]

primary ray
↓
reflection ray
↓
shadow ray
↓
indirect bounce ray

[end plain-text]

A single pixel may generate dozens of rays before the path ends.

So the total ray count can easily reach tens or hundreds of billions for a high-quality render.

5. The Eye Does Not Solve the Rendering Equation

The human eye does not compute global light transport mathematically.

Instead:

– the physical world already solved the light propagation

– photons actually traveled through the environment

– the eye only samples the final result

A renderer must recreate the entire light transport process artificially.

6. Noise Reduction Requires Many Samples

Monte Carlo estimation introduces noise that decreases slowly.

The relationship is:

\text{noise} \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}

This means:

reducing noise by 2×

requires 4× more samples

So high realism quickly becomes computationally expensive.

7. GPUs Handle Massive Parallelism

Graphics processors from companies like NVIDIA and AMD contain thousands of cores.

Each core can trace rays simultaneously:

[plain-text]

GPU core 1 → rays for pixel 1
GPU core 2 → rays for pixel 2
GPU core 3 → rays for pixel 3
...

[end plain-text]

This parallelism allows billions of rays to be simulated relatively quickly.

8. Denoising Algorithms Help

Modern renderers reduce required rays using AI-based denoisers.

For example:

ray tracing generates a noisy image

a neural network estimates the clean image

This allows acceptable results with far fewer samples.

9. A Useful Intuition

Think of rendering like estimating the average brightness of a room by randomly measuring light from different directions.

More measurements produce a more accurate estimate.

The renderer keeps sampling until the estimate converges.

✅ Key takeaway

Even though the human eye has only a few million receptors, a renderer must simulate the entire physics of light transport, which requires sampling vast numbers of possible light paths.

That is why path-traced images often require billions of rays.
